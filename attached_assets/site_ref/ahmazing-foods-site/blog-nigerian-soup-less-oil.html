How to Cook Nigerian Soups With Less Oil (Without Losing Flavour)

AHmazing Foods · Healthy Meals

Palm oil is doing real work in a Nigerian soup — colour, richness, the way it carries pepper heat across your tongue. Cutting it out entirely usually just makes a sadder soup. Cutting it down, deliberately, is a different story.

Start with the pot, not the oil

A lot of oil in traditional cooking is there to stop ingredients from sticking and burning during the initial fry. A good non-stick or well-seasoned pot lets you use noticeably less oil for that first stage without anything catching.

Bloom your pepper and onions in stock, not oil

Instead of frying your pepper mix entirely in oil, try softening it first in a splash of stock or water, then adding a smaller amount of oil at the end just to finish it. You still get the flavour base — you're just not swimming ingredients in oil to get there.

Let vegetables carry more of the dish

Soups that lean heavily on leafy vegetables — ugu, efo, waterleaf — need less oil to feel substantial, because the vegetables themselves add body and volume. A soup that's 60% vegetable by volume simply doesn't need the same oil-to-ingredient ratio as one built mostly around meat and starch.

Skim, don't pour

If you're used to adding oil directly into the pot, try adding slightly less than usual, then finishing with a thin layer skimmed from the top rather than stirred all the way through. You get the visual richness and the top-note flavour with less oil actually absorbed into what you eat.

Pick your battles

Not every soup needs the same treatment. Egusi and ogbono genuinely rely on a certain amount of oil for texture. Okra and vegetable soups are far more forgiving. Reduce where it costs you the least in flavour, and don't fight the soups where oil is structurally part of the dish.

How we apply this

Every stew and soup on our menu can be prepared with reduced oil on request — see our Stews and Soups pages for the specific dishes we'd recommend starting with. This is general cooking guidance, not medical advice — talk to your doctor or a dietitian about what's right for your specific situation.
